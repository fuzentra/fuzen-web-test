For decades, employability felt straightforward: get qualified, get a job, build a career - often within one organisation. Our parents' generation treated employability as a qualification game: earn a respected certificate or diploma, add a degree, collect post-nominals, and move upward. It worked because the world of work was predictable, hierarchical and linear. Careers followed a straight path from education → job → retirement.

But today, that logic is breaking.

We now live in a **Hyper Profiling Paradigm** - where employability is shaped not only by what you have studied, but by how you signal capability, and how effectively you earn trust in public. Yet there is a critical nuance that is often missed in the excitement around personal branding: profiling can project what *looks like* credibility, not what is *credibly earned*. There is a difference between credibility you can portray and credibility you have earned - and the future of employability depends on how we navigate that difference.

Understanding this shift requires looking at the four eras that shaped our reality.

## The Entry Qualification Paradigm

The earliest era of employability emerged when education was scarce, and scarcity made it valuable. In the 1990s and early 2000s, having a qualification - whether a public university degree or a professional path like CIMA or CIM - was enough to define someone as employable. Organisations treated education as a direct proxy for competence, simply because very few possessed it.

The world of work was closed and stable. People joined a company and often stayed for decades. Gen X lived this fully, believing education was a single investment at the start of a career.

This era reflects **Human Capital Theory** - education increases productivity and employability. There was no concept of personal branding, because achievements were visible inside the organisation, not publicly displayed.

## The Degree Paradigm

The second era began when higher education shifted from scarcity to mass accessibility. From the mid-2000s, private universities, foreign affiliations and rising middle-class aspirations expanded access to degrees. A degree became the baseline expectation, and employers used it as a filtering mechanism, even for roles that didn't truly require one.

> The question changed from "Do you have a qualification?" to "Which degree do you have - and from where?"

This reflects **Signalling Theory**, where a degree signals capability rather than guarantees it. The university brand, country affiliation and academic level became differentiators.

Gen Y (Millennials) lived this transition uneasily. They inherited a stability mindset, but globalisation promoted mobility, innovation and entrepreneurship. As degrees multiplied, their differentiation power eroded, standards rose, and the race for uniqueness began.

## The Post-Nominals Paradigm

As degrees multiplied, ambitious professionals turned to credential stacking to stand apart. Post-nominals became the new competitive currency: PhD, MPhil, MBA, MSc, MEng, PGDip, PGCert, FCIM, ACMA.

Education providers encouraged this behaviour, promoting every additional credential as a source of "competitive advantage" and "added value". For many, collecting qualifications became a signalling strategy, rather than a pursuit of deeper capability.

This era exposed a weakness in the qualification logic. More certificates did not guarantee better thinking, stronger execution or meaningful achievement. Employers began assessing talent differently, shifting toward **Knowledge, Skills and Attitude (KSA)** - a competency-based lens that values capability over credentials.

The Post-Nominals Paradigm taught a clear lesson: employability is not a paper-collection game. Eventually, every credential faces the same question: *"Show me what you've actually done."*

## The Profiling Paradigm

Today, we are in a new paradigm driven by digital identity, open knowledge and networked reputation. The internet has democratised learning: anyone can build skills through YouTube, Coursera, LinkedIn Learning, GitHub or community-driven ecosystems. It has also democratised distribution - professionals can showcase capability without waiting for permission or hierarchy.

This has created a world where how you signal competence matters: how you think, what you share, the projects you build, the problems you solve, and how others respond to your ideas. Professionals across generations - Gen Y (Millennials), Gen Z and Alpha - are building digital identities as part of their career strategy. Gen Y pioneered the shift from private CVs to public credibility; Gen Z and Alpha have accelerated it through native digital behaviour. They write content, build communities, collaborate openly and share their thinking.

This reflects **Boundaryless Career Theory** - a career defined not by one employer, but by the strength of one's network, portfolio and visibility.

However, the Profiling Paradigm has a paradox: visibility can create perceived competence, even without real capability behind it. Opinions can look like expertise. Content can look like experience. A curated profile can look like credibility. That is why profiling must be viewed through a filter of proof - a real portfolio, measurable outcomes, earned achievements, recognised contribution, and trust built over time. Without that foundation, profiling becomes a performance of competence, not the presence of it.

Ultimately, the future of employability will not be won by the loudest profile, but by the profile grounded in real substance.

## The business of manufactured credibility

The Profiling Paradigm has also created an industry around perceived credibility. As visibility became a strong signal in the job market, a parallel ecosystem of mushroom organisations emerged - entities that sell awards, titles and recognitions for a fee, often packaged with glamour and ceremony.

These award factories offer glossy trophies, gala nights, staged recognition, celebrity speakers, photo opportunities and media coverage - all positioned as proof of excellence. In reality, the model monetises the desire for visibility. Individuals and companies pay to purchase a narrative of success, which is then amplified online as if it were earned credibility.

The psychology is simple: when audiences scroll fast and judge signals quickly, a medal can look like mastery, and an event photo can look like endorsement. This is where profiling becomes vulnerable. It creates a climate where credibility can be bought, not built - where a shiny award replaces real contribution, industry impact or sustained achievement.

> Crucial questions rarely get asked: Who evaluated the work? What were the selection criteria? Was it merit-based? Is there an expert panel? Or is this simply prestige-for-payment?

When the market rewards appearance over depth, manufactured recognition distorts the Profiling Paradigm, pushing individuals toward theatrics of success rather than substance. Informed employers can easily distinguish real awards from purchased titles.

Real credibility is earned. Perceived credibility is staged. The market will separate the two.

## The question of permanence

As the Profiling Paradigm expands, a strategic question emerges: will profiling create permanence for employers - or does it only generate attention in a noisy market?

A strong online profile may help someone enter the room, but it doesn't guarantee success inside the room. Long-term employability still depends on proven achievements, delivered outcomes and meaningful contribution to teams. Employers ultimately value resilience, judgement, trustworthiness, character and consistency - the maturity required to handle complex work. **Profiling accelerates visibility. Performance creates permanence.**

## A balanced view for employers

Employers are right to look beyond degrees. A public profile can reveal initiative, curiosity and thinking. But they should not purely chase the louder voice, the viral personality or the better storyteller. A curated identity is not the same as credible competence. Hiring should prioritise depth beneath the display: portfolio evidence, impact created, recognition earned, and the ability to solve real problems.

> Profiles can be portrayed. Achievements must be proved.

## The new employability equation

The old model was linear: **Education → Job → Career**.

The new model is a flywheel: **Qualification → Capability → Visibility → Proven Credibility → Opportunity → Permanence**.

Qualification opens the door, capability gives you something to show, visibility gets you noticed, proven credibility wins trust, opportunity comes from the network, and permanence comes from delivered value. A degree may let you enter the building. A profile may get you invited to the table. But your work decides whether you stay.

## Why this shift matters

The Profiling Paradigm reflects deeper changes in how careers are built:

- **Boundaryless careers** - work is portable, not tied to one employer
- **Protean mindset** - success is personally defined, not title-defined
- **Skills-based hiring** - capability drives value, not paper
- **Attention economy** - visibility can mislead; credibility must be earned

In a world where knowledge is abundant and attention is cheap, becoming the benchmark is more valuable than collecting certificates - but only when the benchmark is real.

## Final thought

Don't chase paper. Build capability. Earn credibility. Deliver meaningful results. Become the person others in your industry look up to - not just the person who presents well online.

We have entered a new paradigm: employability is the Profiling Paradigm. But remember: **profiling opens doors. Performance keeps them open.**
