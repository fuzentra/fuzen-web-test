When the Sky Becomes the Network: Sri Lanka's Next Telecom Inflection Point

Low Earth Orbit satellites are reshaping the economics, architecture and sovereignty of national telecoms. What the next 24 months mean for Sri Lanka's operators, regulators and policymakers.

FA

Feroz Ahamed · Co-Founder & Director

2 June 2026 · 7 min read

The satellite connectivity revolution is no longer on the horizon - it is already reshaping the economics, architecture and sovereignty of national telecommunications. Here is what that means for Sri Lanka.

While Sri Lanka was racing to expand 5G coverage, improve customer network experience and accelerate public-sector digitisation, the global connectivity stack itself was being rebuilt - from orbit. The decisions made over the next 24 months by operators, regulators and policymakers in Colombo will likely determine whether Sri Lanka participates in that future as a strategic player, or becomes a passive consumer of infrastructure increasingly controlled elsewhere.

The LEO revolution is already here

Low Earth Orbit (LEO) satellites - typically operating below 1,200 km - have fundamentally altered the economics of satellite connectivity. Unlike traditional geostationary systems positioned nearly 35,000 km away, LEO constellations can deliver latency in the 20-40 ms range, enabling real-time applications that were previously impractical over satellite infrastructure.

SpaceX's Starlink now operates the world's largest active LEO constellation (more than 10,100 satellites in orbit) and serves millions of users globally, making it increasingly difficult to describe satellite broadband as a niche or experimental technology. It has effectively become a new layer of global communications infrastructure.

At the same time, Amazon's Project Kuiper is assembling a competing constellation of more than 3,000 satellites while positioning itself aggressively for the next phase of satellite-terrestrial convergence. Beyond the US-led race, China's state-backed LEO initiatives are also accelerating, reinforcing the reality that satellite connectivity is becoming part of a broader geopolitical and digital infrastructure contest across Asia and beyond.

The market itself is evolving even faster than many operators anticipated. Direct-to-Device (D2D) connectivity - where satellites communicate directly with standard smartphones without additional hardware - is emerging as the next major structural shift in telecom.

D2D changes the relationship between satellites and telcos

Enterprise broadband is only one part of the story. D2D connectivity changes the relationship between satellites and Mobile Network Operators (MNOs) much more fundamentally. T-Mobile and Starlink have already launched commercial D2D services in the United States, while AT&T, Verizon and Vodafone are pursuing similar capabilities through AST SpaceMobile.

This creates a structural transition in the telecom stack. Satellites are no longer merely alternatives to terrestrial networks; they are increasingly becoming the fallback coverage layer that operators depend on to extend rural connectivity, support maritime and logistics operations, strengthen disaster resilience, and meet universal service obligations that terrestrial economics alone often cannot justify.

The strategic implication is significant because satellite providers are embedding themselves deeper into the telecom value chain while gradually accumulating leverage through infrastructure dependency. Operators that fail to define a clear Non-Terrestrial Network (NTN) strategy risk slowly losing strategic relevance in the connectivity ecosystem they once fully controlled.

Sri Lanka is entering an inflection point

Sri Lanka's telecom sector has made meaningful progress despite years of economic instability and regulatory delays. Dialog Axiata PLC and SLT-Mobitel commercially launched 5G services following long-delayed spectrum allocation in the 3.5 GHz and 27 GHz bands, while the country itself now enjoys near-universal 4G population coverage, one of the world's lowest mobile data costs, and a highly mobile-first consumer base.

On the satellite front, Starlink entered Sri Lanka commercially in 2025 following regulatory approval by the Telecommunications Regulatory Commission of Sri Lanka. More strategically important, however, was Dialog's partnership with SpaceX to distribute Starlink services locally, positioning LEO connectivity as a complement to terrestrial infrastructure rather than a direct competitive threat.

That instinct is strategically correct, although the broader industry and regulatory framework still need to evolve much further.

The sovereignty question cannot be ignored

Sri Lanka's early hesitation around Starlink was not irrational obstructionism. Concerns surrounding lawful intercept capability, traffic visibility and data sovereignty are legitimate national security questions when communications infrastructure increasingly operates outside traditional jurisdictional boundaries.

At the same time, domestic operators that invested heavily in regulated local infrastructure spent years navigating licensing complexity, taxation obligations and spectrum delays, while foreign satellite operators moved comparatively quickly through approval pathways. That imbalance has implications not only for investment confidence, but also for the long-term sovereignty of national digital infrastructure.

Sri Lanka therefore needs a regulatory architecture that enables LEO participation while preserving meaningful local anchoring through in-country gateway infrastructure, lawful intercept capability via licensed domestic entities, transparent spectrum governance, and clearly defined satellite-terrestrial integration frameworks.

Countries that establish these frameworks early negotiate from strength, while countries that delay often inherit standards and operating conditions designed elsewhere.

The financial reality facing operators

Sri Lanka's leading telecom operators are financially healthier today than they have been in several years, supported by disciplined cost management, infrastructure modernisation and post-crisis balance-sheet recovery. However, the structural pressures beneath those financial improvements remain substantial.

The economics of 5G rollout continue to challenge operators globally because dense site deployments, fibre backhaul requirements, energy costs and spectrum investments create capital intensity significantly higher than previous network generations, while consumer ARPU growth remains comparatively limited.

Sri Lanka's ultra-low mobile data pricing accelerates adoption and digital inclusion, but it simultaneously compresses infrastructure returns at a time when network modernisation costs continue rising. With subscriber growth nearing saturation, future revenue expansion will increasingly depend on enterprise services, digital platforms, infrastructure partnerships and premium connectivity offerings rather than traditional subscriber acquisition.

This is precisely where satellite integration becomes strategically important. The question is no longer "mobile versus satellite", but rather which connectivity layer is economically optimal for a specific geography, density profile and use case.

Terrestrial 5G will continue to dominate dense urban environments due to superior throughput, latency and cost-per-bit efficiency. However, beyond certain geographic and population-density thresholds, terrestrial economics weaken rapidly, making LEO connectivity increasingly viable for rural coverage, offshore operations, logistics corridors, fisheries and disaster recovery scenarios.

The winning architecture is therefore unlikely to be replacement; it will be hybridisation. Dialog's Starlink partnership reflects precisely this logic by leveraging satellite infrastructure without absorbing the full satellite capex burden internally.

The bottlenecks ahead

The LEO transition is real, but it is not frictionless.

  • Spectrum congestion is becoming increasingly complex as thousands of satellites are deployed across competing constellations, creating growing challenges around coordination and interference management. Smaller markets risk becoming passive recipients of global spectrum decisions unless regulators actively participate in international coordination frameworks.
  • Ground infrastructure remains a critical gap. Sri Lanka currently lacks domestic gateway infrastructure for major LEO operators, meaning traffic routing occurs through external gateways, creating both performance and sovereignty concerns. Over time, local gateway infrastructure may become one of the most strategically important digital assets in the country.
  • Regulatory capacity is another challenge because satellite-terrestrial convergence is moving faster than many telecom regulators were originally designed to handle. The TRCSL will require deeper technical and policy capability in NTN governance, D2D licensing, spectrum coordination and cross-border satellite operations if it hopes to remain ahead of the market rather than reacting to it.
  • Device readiness will also shape adoption timelines. While D2D connectivity works on standard smartphones, compatibility still depends on specific modem and handset capabilities that remain concentrated in newer devices. In price-sensitive markets like Sri Lanka, this will likely delay mass-market adoption even after commercial services become available.

Five strategic priorities for Sri Lanka

  1. Satellite-terrestrial partnerships should become a regulated industry pathway rather than isolated bilateral arrangements, giving every licensed operator clarity around satellite integration, D2D participation and service reselling frameworks.
  2. 5G investments should be designed with NTN readiness from the outset, because 3GPP standards already support integrated terrestrial and non-terrestrial architectures.
  3. LEO connectivity should be deployed strategically to close rural, maritime and disaster-resilience gaps that terrestrial economics alone are unlikely to solve efficiently.
  4. Sri Lanka should establish a D2D regulatory framework before market pressure forces reactive policymaking through roaming agreements, device ecosystems and cross-border service expansion.
  5. Digital sovereignty should be treated as infrastructure policy rather than a narrow regulatory debate. Sri Lanka should negotiate actively around local gateway presence, spectrum value, disaster resilience obligations, local capability development and regulatory visibility into critical communications infrastructure.

The window is narrowing

Sri Lanka occupies a uniquely important position, with major maritime trade routes, a digitally engaged population, growing enterprise digitisation and genuine ambitions around digital transformation. The satellite connectivity revolution - led by Starlink, Project Kuiper, AST SpaceMobile and emerging Asian players - is not inherently a threat to those ambitions. Properly integrated, it could accelerate national connectivity, economic inclusion and digital resilience faster than traditional telecom models alone could realistically achieve.

However, the value created by that transition will not distribute automatically. It will favour countries that engage early, regulate intelligently and negotiate strategically, rather than waiting passively for global infrastructure models to arrive fully defined.

The sky is becoming the network, and the question for Sri Lanka is whether we build a roof - or a launchpad.
